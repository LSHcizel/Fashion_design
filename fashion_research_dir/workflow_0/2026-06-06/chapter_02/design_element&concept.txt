Theme: Sous le Salon la Plage
Sub-Theme/Chapter: The Wardrobe That Crosses the Shore
==================================================
**Candidate elements/concepts**

1. Shoreline Tailoring | Cropped Bleu de Travail Jacket | 18%
A compact, boxy jacket that translates French workwear into Chanel-level precision. The silhouette should stop at the high hip, with strong shoulder clarity, clean topstitching, and practical patch pockets, balancing utility and elegance. Use washed navy cotton canvas, softened black twill, or faded denim-blue twill with matte horn buttons and subtle pearl-finished metal snaps. The color story should stay disciplined—navy, ink black, pale denim blue, and chalk white—so the garment reads as authoritative, wearable, and coastal without becoming costume.

2. Shoreline Tailoring | Relaxed Sailor Trouser
A fluid trouser designed for movement, with a slightly eased waist, gentle taper, and rolled cuff that feels suited to deck, terrace, and city alike. The fit should be relaxed but controlled, with sharp front creases and functional pocketing to preserve polish. Materials such as lightweight wool gabardine, washed canvas, and technical silk blends can keep the silhouette crisp while allowing ease. Color should echo maritime codes: deep navy, sea-black, salt white, and weathered stone grey.

3. Shoreline Tailoring | Wrap-Front Utility Top
This piece merges a chore-shirt logic with a refined wrap closure, creating a practical but sensual layer that can be worn over swimwear or tailored bottoms. The neckline should open cleanly at the collarbone, with a side-tie or hidden button placket that allows adaptable styling. Fine cotton drill, silk-cotton poplin, or brushed twill would make the concept feasible and comfortable. Colors should favor faded indigo, soft ecru, sandy taupe, and black with white contrast piping.

4. Crossing Layers | Shirt-Dress Over Swim
A key hybrid that captures the collection’s salon-to-beach transition: a striped shirt-dress worn open, belted, or partially buttoned over a swimsuit. The garment should have a straight, lightly oversized cut with a crisp collar, cuffed sleeves, and hem length that can function as both cover-up and day dress. Use fine cotton shirting, voile, or a technical silk-cotton mix to keep it light and breathable. The palette should center on Basque stripes in navy and white, with optional accents of shell pink or pale aqua for subtle coastal brightness.  

5. Crossing Layers | Tuxedo Beach Robe
A long, robe-like outer layer that borrows the formality of eveningwear while staying relaxed enough for seaside movement. The silhouette should be fluid and elongated, with a soft shoulder, deep lapel, and sash belt to suggest both dressing gown and evening coat. Washed canvas, silk faille, or light double-face wool can give the necessary body without heaviness. Colors should sit in elegant contrast: black, midnight navy, ivory, and faint silver sheen, making the piece feel like black-tie codes dissolved by sea air.

6. Crossing Layers | Belted Cardigan Jacket | 14%
A pivotal piece for the chapter, this cardigan-jacket hybrid embodies Chanel structure softened for motion. It should be slightly cropped or hip-length, with knit body construction, stable shoulder shaping, and a self-belt or chain-weighted belt to define the waist without rigidity. A dense knit, tweed-knit, or compact boucle would be feasible while retaining the house’s tactile identity. Color should move between cream, black, navy, and pale pearl grey, with tiny metallic highlights or braid edges to reinforce couture discipline.

7. Crossing Layers | Swim-Layer Bodysuit
A clean, swimsuit-based underlayer designed to function beneath tailoring or as a visible base in its own right. The shape should be sculpted but not restrictive, with a high or moderate neckline, clean leg line, and minimal seams for comfort and practical wearability. Performance jersey, bonded swim fabric, and matte stretch microfiber are appropriate materials for technical feasibility. Colors should stay close to the house palette: black, deep navy, white, shell, and muted lagoon blue, allowing the piece to anchor more layered looks.

8. Surface and Handfeel | Washed Canvas Finish
This category defines the tactile foundation of the chapter, using canvas that feels weathered by sun and salt rather than stiff or industrial. Garments in this finish should have softened edges, broken-in drape, and visible seam structure to suggest use and mobility. The material is highly feasible for jackets, robes, trousers, and skirts, especially when softened through garment washing. Color treatments should appear sun-faded: sandy beige, oyster white, washed navy, smoke grey, and chalky black.

9. Surface and Handfeel | Fine Breton Stripe Knit
A signature coastal graphic translated into a wearable knit that can serve as top, dress, or cover-up. The stripe should be regular but not overly literal, with enough scale variation to feel modern and avoid cliché. Rib knit, cotton-viscose blend, or lightweight wool-silk knit would support comfortable layering and clean body lines. Colors should be crisp and coastal: navy and white, black and ivory, or diluted blue and cream, possibly with a silver thread for controlled shimmer.

10. Surface and Handfeel | Salt-Sheened Tweed
A Chanel cornerstone reimagined with a coastal, light-reflective finish that suggests sun on seawater. The tweed should remain structured enough for jackets and skirts, but incorporate lighter yarns, open texture, or metallic filament to reduce weight and increase fluidity. This is technically feasible for resort dressing if kept compact and breathable. Color should blend oyster white, pearl grey, navy flecks, black flecks, and subtle silver sparkle, creating an elegant surface tension between craft and atmosphere.

11. Finishing Codes | Nautical Hardware Trims
Small metal details should carry the marine narrative without overwhelming the garments: polished snaps, tab closures, strap rings, and restrained buckle forms inspired by deck equipment. These elements should be integrated into pockets, belts, cuffs, and shoulder tabs, lending functional clarity and subtle shine. Finish them in brushed silver, pale pewter, or pearlized white metal to remain coherent with the coastal palette. The effect should be precise and luxurious, not overtly nautical.

12. Finishing Codes | Pearled Edge and Shell-Glow Accents
A softer finishing language that references Chanel’s jewelry and the light of the shore through delicate, wearable detail. Use pearl-bead edging, shell-like buttons, or iridescent topstitch highlights sparingly so the collection feels refined rather than ornate. These accents work best on knitwear, collars, cuffs, and skirt hems, where they can catch light with movement. Color should remain understated: ivory pearl, opalescent white, pale blush, and translucent silver, adding a quiet luminous finish to the darker workwear base.
